The words we leave behind

We remember the words that hurt us. How often do we remember the words we've used to hurt others?

Words have shaped my life in surprising ways.

Holding a microphone as MC at a family wedding led me to the woman I married. Speaking live on BBC Radio Cambridgeshire, years after being the quiet one in every room, taught me that a voice can grow.

And in the consulting room, I've learned that how we speak can matter as much as what we say.

Explaining a diagnosis is different from simply delivering information. A frightened patient needs to feel heard, not just informed.

Yet so often, the people we love most receive the least careful version of us. A careless remark at home. A sharp tone after a long day. A silence that punishes.

In Sahaja Yoga, the Vishuddhi is associated with communication, diplomacy and our relationships with others.

Before speaking, I try, not always successfully, to ask three questions. Is it true? Is it helpful? Can I say it without wounding someone's dignity?

Diplomacy isn't dishonesty. Kindness doesn't mean avoiding difficult truths. It means not making someone's dignity the price of our honesty.

Meditation helps me create a little space between what I feel and what I say.

Our words last only a moment. Their echoes can last a lifetime.
