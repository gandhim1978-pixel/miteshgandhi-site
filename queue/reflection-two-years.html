Turning Points · 08

The two years that rerouted my life

It began with a moment of kindness. And a mistake.

Some years ago, a young man sat in my consulting chair. He was stressed about his exams, carrying the quiet weight that so many teenagers carry. His mother was elsewhere in the store.

I wanted to help him.

So I told him about the thing that had helped me most in my own darkest days: Sahaja Yoga meditation, and the calm it can bring.

My heart was in the right place. My judgement was not.

He was young. His parent wasn't there. And I was his optometrist, not his teacher.

When she heard about it, she was worried. Looking back, I understand exactly why.

A complaint followed, to the General Optical Council, the body that regulates my profession.

I had two young children and a wife to support. And suddenly, everything I had built, my qualifications, my reputation, my livelihood, felt as fragile as glass.

I have rarely felt so helpless.

What followed was nearly two years of waiting.

Two years of letters and questions. Two years of lying awake at night, replaying a single conversation. Two years of wondering what people would think if they knew.

I questioned everything. My career. My judgement. Even, on the hardest nights, my faith.

All I wanted was to share Mother's gift. Why had it led here?

During that time, I could no longer work at the practice where I'd spent years. The comfortable routine, the free parking, the short drive home: all of it was gone overnight.

So I had to look elsewhere.

I took one day a week at a small independent practice in Cambridge called Clamp. I began teaching optometry students at Anglia Ruskin University. I met people I would never otherwise have met.

I didn't choose any of it bravely. I was pushed.

Near the end of those two years, we took a family holiday to Switzerland. We stayed in a chalet looking out at the Matterhorn, which Sahaja yogis revere as a natural, self-manifested form of Shri Ganesha. For a few days, in front of that mountain, I simply felt held.

Soon after we came home, my phone rang. It was my wife.

"Mitesh, I've got some good news for you."

After two years of waiting, good news could have meant anything. A tenant. A purchase. Something about our health.

"No," she said. "This is going to be the best news you've ever, ever wanted."

The letter had arrived. I was cleared on all accounts. No action was taken. Nothing was recorded against my name.

The release I felt in that moment was like nothing since the day I finally qualified as an optometrist. Two years of weight, lifted in a single sentence.

I don't think it's a coincidence that it came straight after standing before the Matterhorn.

And then life began to unfold in ways I could never have planned. Within a year, I was a director at Clamp. Awards followed. I returned to study at 42 and became a prescribing optometrist. I found a team I love, and work that gives me purpose, freedom and choices I never had as a locum.

Looking back now, I see those two years completely differently.

What felt like punishment was redirection.

I've made many raw decisions in my life. Leaving home, unwell and uncertain, for India. Saying yes to a stranger's spare key. Staying far too long somewhere comfortable. Some felt foolish at the time. Some were genuinely wrong. This one was wrong.

And yet every single one has led me somewhere I needed to be.

I don't believe that is chance. I believe Mother's hand was in all of it, even in the moments I couldn't feel it. Especially in those.

I've also learned something that I hope every yogi will hold close.

Sahaja Yoga is the greatest gift I know, and I still long for everyone to discover it. But it must be shared with wisdom: in the right setting, at the right time, and with a parent's knowledge when someone is young. Never with someone who has come to me for something else. My patients come to me for their eyes, and their trust deserves protecting. My meditation is now offered elsewhere, freely, to anyone who chooses it.

I don't share this story because it ended well.

I share it because, in the middle of it, I couldn't see how it possibly could.

If you're in that middle right now, unable to see the way through, I hope this helps you trust a little. You are being carried, even when it doesn't feel that way.

Which of your "mistakes" might one day turn out to be your turning point?
