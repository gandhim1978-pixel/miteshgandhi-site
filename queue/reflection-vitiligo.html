What vitiligo taught me about being seen

When people meet me, they see the suit, the smile, the practice.

They don't always know that I grew up with vitiligo, a condition that removes pigment from patches of the skin.

As a boy, I knew exactly what it felt like to be looked at. Not seen. Looked at.

The glance that lingers a moment too long. The question asked a little too loudly. The feeling of wanting to disappear.

Vitiligo ran in my family. My grandmother lived with it too, in a time and place where it carried real stigma. She went from temple to temple searching for comfort. I think of her often.

For years, I tried to hide. Long sleeves. Careful angles. Quiet corners.

Then something shifted. I realised that the people who mattered most weren't looking at my skin. They were looking at me.

That's where my whole philosophy of care began.

Every person who walks into my consulting room carries something they're worried others will notice. A nervous habit. A difficult past. A fear about their eyes. A body they're uncomfortable in.

My job isn't just to examine their eyes. It's to make them feel seen, not looked at.

There's a difference, and anyone who has ever felt different knows it immediately.

Being looked at makes us smaller. Being seen gives us back our size.

I'm grateful now for the boy who learned that lesson early. He taught the man how to care.
