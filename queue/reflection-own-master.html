Becoming our own master

It's possible to know a great deal about life and still struggle to guide your own.

I grew up deferring to a strong father. For a long time, I looked to others to tell me what was right, what was safe, what I should do next.

Then life asked me to find my own way.

In Sahaja Yoga, the Void represents the principle of the inner guru: developing our own discernment, mastery and capacity to guide ourselves.

Teaching optometry students at Anglia Ruskin has taught me something about this. The best students aren't the ones who memorise answers. They're the ones who learn to think, to question, and to admit when they're unsure.

Good clinical judgement depends on observation, evidence and a willingness to reconsider. I think spirituality deserves the same honesty.

Can I question my own motives? Can I admit when I'm wrong? Do I follow a principle because it's right, or because it's comfortable?

In my pottery classes, I'm learning how hard it is to centre clay on the wheel. Push too hard and it collapses. Too little and it wobbles. Mastery is a quiet, steady pressure from the centre.

Becoming our own master doesn't mean controlling others or never making mistakes. It means taking responsibility for ourselves.

Meditation gives space to observe our reactions. Introspection helps us recognise our patterns. Daily life gives us endless chances to change them.
